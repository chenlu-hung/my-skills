# English AI tell list

For **DOC** only. CHAT does not need a line-by-line sweep; CODE is exempt entirely.

**These are tendencies, not bans.** Test: if removing the word changes what the
sentence means, keep it. Prose with no adverbs, no dashes, and uniform sentence
length has its own detectable signature, and it reads worse than the tell.

## Throat-clearing openers

The writer needs the runway. The reader does not.

- "Here's the thing:" and every other "here's what / here's why / here's how"
- "It turns out"
- "The truth is" / "The uncomfortable truth is"
- "Let me be clear"
- "I'll be honest"
- "The real question is"
- "In today's [X] landscape"
- "At its core"
- "It's worth noting that"

## Emphasis crutches

These add stress, not meaning.

- "Full stop." / "Period."
- "Let that sink in."
- "Make no mistake"
- "This matters because" (say why; the announcement is redundant)
- "Here's why that matters"

## Binary contrast

The most reliable single tell. A negation, then the reversal.

| Pattern | Fix |
|---|---|
| "It's not X, it's Y" | State Y |
| "The question isn't X. It's Y." | Ask Y |
| "X isn't the problem. Y is." | "Y is the problem" |
| "not just X but Y" | Pick the one that carries the point |
| "It feels like X. It's actually Y." | State Y |
| "stops being X and starts being Y" | Say what it is now |

## Negative listing

"Not a framework. Not a library. A convention." Three negations to set up one noun.
Write the noun.

## Dramatic fragmentation

- "[Noun]. That's it. That's the whole trick."
- "X. And Y. And Z."
- A one-word sentence for emphasis.

Fragments are fine in list items, table cells, and labels. As a rhetorical device
they read as manufactured profundity.

## Rhetorical setups

- "What if we thought about it differently?"
- "Here's what I mean:"
- "Think about it:"
- "And that's okay."

Make the point; let the reader draw the conclusion.

## False agency

Inanimate subjects doing human verbs. This shows up because it lets the writer skip
naming an actor.

| Pattern | Who actually did it |
|---|---|
| "the data tells us" | Someone read it and concluded something |
| "the decision emerges" | Someone decided |
| "the culture shifts" | People changed behavior |
| "a complaint becomes a fix" | An engineer fixed it |
| "the market rewards" | Buyers paid |
| "this design unlocks" | Users can now do a specific thing |

Name the person. When no specific person fits, use "you" and put the reader in the
seat.

## Narrator from a distance

- "Nobody designed this."
- "People tend to..."
- "This is why..."

Put the reader in the room instead.

## Business jargon

| Avoid | Use |
|---|---|
| navigate (challenges) | handle, address |
| unpack | explain, examine |
| lean into | accept, commit to |
| landscape | situation, field |
| game-changer | say what changed |
| double down | commit further |
| deep dive | analysis |
| circle back | return to |
| moving forward | next |
| leverage (verb) | use |

## Filler adverbs and hedges

really, just, simply, actually, literally, genuinely, honestly, truly,
fundamentally, inherently, inevitably, interestingly, importantly, crucially,
arguably

Delete on sight unless the word carries a real distinction. "Technically correct"
and "correct" mean different things; keep that one.

## Rhythm

- Three consecutive sentences of similar length. Break one.
- The rule of three in every list. Two items are usually the honest count.
- Every paragraph ending on a punchy one-liner.
- Anything that reads like a pull-quote. Rewrite it into the paragraph.

## Punctuation

- More than one em dash in a paragraph. One is fine; a habit is a tell.
- Exclamation marks in technical prose.
- Bold on the first phrase of every bullet.
- Section headers with emoji.

## Vague declaratives

"The implications are significant." "The reasons are structural." "This has
far-reaching effects."

Name the implication, the reason, the effect. If you cannot name it, you do not have
the sentence yet.
